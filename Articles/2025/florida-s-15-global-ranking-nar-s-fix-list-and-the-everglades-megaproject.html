Florida’s #15 Global Ranking, NAR’s Fix List, and the Everglades Megaproject

Author: Bonita Springs-Estero REALTORS® • Published 2025-11-12

Florida’s economy has reached a historic milestone. The Florida Chamber Foundation announced that the Sunshine State’s gross domestic product now exceeds $1.7 trillion, which means that if Florida were an independent nation it would rank 15th largest in the world. Florida recently surpassed Spain in GDP and is closing in on Australia (just $25.5 billion behind). State leaders have long aimed to make Florida a top-10 global economy by 2030, and this latest jump (up from 18th in 2017 to 15th today) shows steady progress toward that goal. “When we launched the Florida 2030 Blueprint, Florida ranked 18th in the world,” notes Florida Chamber President Mark Wilson. “Today, we are 15th and gaining ground on Australia. This is the direct result of years of laser focus on free enterprise, principled leadership, smart policy decisions and business and community leaders working together to secure Florida’s future.”

According to a preview of the Chamber Foundation’s upcoming Florida 2030 Blueprint Halftime Report, Florida’s economic growth has been driven by strong job creation and population gains. Over the past five years, the state added many high-wage jobs and expanded its workforce to over 10 million workers, which is more than the population of 40 U.S. states. At the same time, Florida has 155,742 fewer children in poverty thanks in part to initiatives like the Florida Prosperity Initiative. The report also highlights several categories where Florida leads the nation, including:

-#1 in new business start-ups. Entrepreneurial activity is booming in Florida.

-#1 higher education system. Florida’s state university system consistently ranks at the top for affordability and quality.

-#1 in manufacturing job growth. Florida has added manufacturing jobs at the fastest rate in the country.

-#1 in net income migration. No state attracts more net wealth from new residents than Florida.

-#1 in new business relocations. Companies are moving to Florida in record numbers.

-#1 in Black-owned businesses. Florida has the highest number of Black-owned businesses nationwide.

-#2 in Hispanic- and women-owned businesses. Florida is a leader in minority and women entrepreneurship.

Lowest state debt per capita. Prudent fiscal management has kept Florida’s per-person state debt the lowest in the U.S.

Florida Chamber of Commerce

Top tier for infrastructure and talent growth. The state’s investments in infrastructure and workforce are among the best in the country, helping sustain its rapid growth.

Housing Affordability Crisis Spurs NAR to Act

An increasing share of equity-rich repeat buyers are making all-cash purchases, while many first-time buyers struggle to enter the market – creating what one expert calls a “tale of two cities” in today’s housing market.

Even as Florida’s economy surges, fewer Floridians are able to achieve the dream of homeownership in the current market. A new report from the National Association of REALTORS® (NAR) highlights a growing affordability crisis: over the past year, first-time home buyers made up just 21% of all buyers – an all-time low. The typical first-time buyer is now 40 years old, the oldest on record (up from age 33 just five years ago). By contrast, before 2008 first-time buyers represented about 40% of the market, but today they’re only one-fifth. NAR Executive Vice President Shannon McGahn warns that “the shrinking path to homeownership threatens the foundation of upward mobility itself,” noting that the average homeowner’s net wealth is now 43 times that of the average renter. The gap between owners and renters is growing, raising alarms about long-term equity and wealth inequality. McGahn explains that if homeownership is delayed from age 30 to 40, a family could lose out on roughly $150,000 in equity they might have built in a starter home – a significant setback in wealth accumulation.

To address these challenges, NAR is pressuring policymakers at all levels to enact solutions that improve housing affordability and access. “We need solutions that encourage more owners to sell, revitalize underused properties, streamline local zoning and permitting barriers, and modernize construction methods to build more homes faster and more affordably,” McGahn says. In conjunction with NAR’s latest report, the association’s advocacy arm released a comprehensive “menu of solutions” – a set of policy principles and legislative ideas designed to ease the affordability crunch. These include incentives for local governments to reform restrictive zoning and permitting laws that limit new housing development, expanded down payment assistance for first-time and first-generation buyers, tax incentives to help buyers save for a down payment, and support for assumable mortgages and other financing innovations. NAR is also urging stronger enforcement of fair housing laws and the expansion of homeownership education programs.

On Capitol Hill, several bipartisan bills have been introduced to tackle different facets of the housing affordability problem, and NAR is actively backing them:

  • ROAD to Housing Act: A broad federal effort to identify and remove barriers preventing families from becoming homeowners.
  • More Homes on the Market Act (H.R. 1340): Would reduce the “equity penalty” for longtime owners who sell (by raising the capital gains tax exclusion), encouraging more move-up sellers to put homes on the market.
  • Uplifting First-Time Homebuyers Act (H.R. 3526): Would allow first-time buyers to withdraw more money penalty-free from IRAs to use toward a down payment.
  • Housing Supply Framework Act (H.R. 2840/S. 1299): Would require a national strategy to boost housing construction and reduce barriers to development, addressing the supply shortage at its root.

“Tale of Two Cities” in Today’s Housing Market

NAR’s 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers reveals a housing market increasingly split between equity-rich repeat buyers and cash-strapped first-time buyers. “Unfolding in the housing market is a tale of two cities,” says NAR deputy chief economist Jessica Lautz. “We’re seeing buyers with significant housing equity making larger down payments and all-cash offers, while first-time buyers continue to struggle to enter the market.” This dynamic is reshaping who is buying and how they are buying. Below are 10 striking trends from NAR’s new report (based on transactions from July 2024 through June 2025) that illustrate these extremes:

  • First-time buyers at record lows: First-time homebuyers comprised only 21% of recent buyers – the lowest share since NAR began tracking in 1981. High prices, limited inventory, and rising rents made it tough for new buyers to enter the market. Those who did buy are generally older than before.
  • Age of first-time buyers hits 40: The median age of a first-time buyer is now 40, an all-time high (up from the mid-20s a few decades ago). Younger buyers are delaying homeownership due to student debt, high rents, and other financial hurdles, meaning a lost decade of wealth-building for many.
  • Record high of all-cash buyers: Around 26% of home purchases in the past year were all-cash deals – a record high. By comparison, in the early 2000s fewer than 1 in 10 buyers paid all cash. Today’s cash buyers are often leveraging equity from a prior home sale and avoiding high mortgage rates.
  • Higher down payments: Buyers are putting more money down. In 2025 the median down payment reached 19% of the purchase price – the highest in decades. First-time buyers put a median 10% down, while repeat buyers put 23% down. These are the highest levels for first-timers since 1989 and for repeat buyers since 2003, reflecting how much equity repeat buyers have and how long first-timers save.
  • Real estate agents remain essential: 88% of buyers purchased through a real estate agent or broker, and 91% of home sellers used an agent – the highest rate on record. Despite the DIY tech tools available, most consumers value an agent’s expertise in pricing, marketing, negotiation and navigating the process. For-sale-by-owner transactions fell to just 5% of sales, an all-time low, and those FSBO homes tended to sell for significantly less than agent-assisted sales.
  • Repeat buyers flex financial muscle: Today’s repeat buyers are generally older (median age 62) and wealthier than past generations, and they’re dominating the market. The repeat-buyer share is about 79% (the flip side of the 21% first-timer share). The typical repeat buyer made a 23% down payment and nearly one in three bought with all cash, bypassing financing entirely. Years of home equity gains have supercharged their buying power – the average homeowner gained $140,900 in equity in just the last five years. Many long-time owners can easily afford to “move up” or buy second homes, outcompeting younger buyers.
  • Fewer families with children buying: The share of homebuyers who have kids under 18 has dropped to 24%, the lowest on record. In 1985, by contrast, 58% of buyers had children at home. Today’s buyers include more single adults and childless couples. (Single women made up 21% of recent buyers and single men 9%, both growing segments.) Factors like later marriage/childbirth, high childcare costs, and the surge of baby boomer buyers mean fewer “traditional” family households are in the market.
  • Homebuyers and sellers are older than ever: Across the board, Americans are moving later in life. The median age of all home buyers hit 59, and for home sellers it’s about 64 – both the highest ever recorded. Baby Boomers now represent the largest share of buyers and sellers, often because they are in a better financial position to move. This “graying” of the market is a big shift from past decades and is tied to longer life expectancies and the fact that many younger adults are renting longer.
  • Buying for the long haul: People who do buy a home plan to stay put much longer than before. The median expected tenure for buyers in their new home is now 15 years – up sharply from the 6–7 year median tenure seen in the early 2000s. In fact, 28% of buyers say their new home is their “forever” home that they never intend to leave. This trend is reinforced by owners who refinanced or bought at ultra-low interest rates and feel “locked in” to their current home. It also reflects the older age of buyers (skipping the starter home) and a shift in mindset toward housing as a long-term investment. Notably, the typical seller in 2025 had owned their home for 11 years before selling – also a record high, indicating homeowners are staying put longer.
  • New home purchases tick up: With resale inventory so tight, more buyers are turning to newly built homes. New construction accounted for 16% of recent home purchases, a slight uptick that matches levels last seen in 2006. Home builders have helped draw buyers by offering incentives like price cuts and mortgage rate buydowns. Buyers cite the appeal of avoiding renovation projects and getting modern features as reasons to buy new, whereas those preferring existing homes often point to the lower prices and the charm/character of older houses.

Everglades Restoration Reaches New Milestone

In environmental news, Florida marked a major achievement this week in the ongoing effort to restore the Everglades. Governor Ron DeSantis traveled to Palm Beach County to break ground on a massive new inflow pump station for the Everglades Agricultural Area (EAA) Reservoir project. This reservoir and pump station are critical pieces of Florida’s strategy to re-establish the natural water flow from Lake Okeechobee southward into the Everglades – a flow that was disrupted by decades of flood-control projects. Once completed, the EAA Reservoir (spanning 10,000 acres) will be able to store over 78 billion gallons of water (about as much as 120,000 Olympic swimming pools) and deliver up to 470 billion gallons of clean water per year into the Everglades and Florida Bay. That volume of water – made possible by the new pump station’s nine massive pumps moving 3 billion gallons per day – will help hydrate parched wetlands, recharge the Biscayne Aquifer (South Florida’s main source of drinking water), and significantly reduce the polluted discharges that have plagued the Caloosahatchee and St. Lucie estuaries.

For South Florida residents, this means a healthier environment and improved water quality. “The EAA Reservoir is a critical component of Everglades restoration,” said Florida Department of Environmental Protection Secretary Alexis Lambert, “and [Governor DeSantis] has led the charge on getting this project done. Restoring America’s Everglades is one of the most ambitious environmental restoration projects ever undertaken and will restore the natural flow of clean water south to where it’s needed most.” Ecologists have long called for redirecting Lake Okeechobee’s overflow south (into the Everglades) rather than east and west (into canals that discharge to coastal waters). The new reservoir and pump system will finally make that possible, sending more fresh water into Everglades National Park and dramatically cutting the harmful releases that have contributed to algae blooms in the Caloosahatchee and St. Lucie rivers.

Equally significant is how fast this project is moving. Thanks to an agreement between Florida and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, the EAA Reservoir’s timeline has been accelerated by five years – it’s now on track for completion by 2029 instead of 2034. This puts the state ahead of schedule on what is often called the “crown jewel” of Everglades restoration. Officials noted that this week’s groundbreaking was the 80th Everglades restoration milestone event since 2019 – reflecting an unprecedented pace of progress in the last several years. Billions of dollars in state funding (over $3.3 billion in the first term of the DeSantis administration, and another $4.6 billion committed since 2023) have supercharged efforts to get key projects designed, built, and operational.

Local environmental groups and civic leaders cheered the EAA pump station groundbreaking as “a pivotal moment” for Florida’s environment. Once the reservoir and its pump become operational, communities from the Everglades down to Florida Bay stand to see lasting benefits – from revived wetlands and fisheries to improved drinking water security and flood protection. With this momentum, Florida’s decades-long quest to save the Everglades is tangibly closer to the finish line.
